How to prepare

  1. Look back through your exercise book at the tasks and questions from your lessons.
  2. Missed a lesson? Ask your teacher, or someone in your class, what you missed and catch up.
  3. Read the Revision tab and do the quiz at the end of each part.
  4. Learn your key words in the Flashcards.
  5. Use Oak National Academy KS3 Religious Education for extra lessons.

To get the very top marks

  1. Answer every question. Never leave one blank: if you are not sure, take your best guess.
  2. Read every question carefully and think about which answer you choose.
  3. Know the definitions of all the key words. Practise them in the Flashcards.
  4. Don’t just memorise the stories. Know the different meanings and interpretations of them.
  5. Use what you learned in Years 7 and 8. For example, link Sikhi to Hindu Dharma and Islam, or Divine Command Theory to the covenant with Abraham.
  6. For written answers, use PEEL. Point: answer the question. Evidence: a belief, teaching, thinker or example. Explain: why it matters. Link back to the question. For “evaluate” questions, add a paragraph that starts However, and finish with a judgement.

Part 1

What was India like in the 15th century?

Theology and Social Sciences · Equality in Sikhi

Key words

  • Gupta Era: a period in India from the 3rd to the 6th century, full of art, literature and knowledge.
  • Mongols: an empire led by Genghis Khan in the 13th and 14th centuries.
  • Mughals: an empire founded by Babur in the 16th century, which ruled much of India.
  • Empire: a group of lands ruled by one ruler or state.

Why does this matter for Sikhi?

  • Sikhi began in the Punjab, in northern India, with Guru Nanak.
  • Hindu Dharma and Islam were the two main religions at the time.
  • Guru Nanak’s ideas were shaped by both traditions, but Sikhi is its own religion.
Remember: Sikhi was born in India, where Hindu Dharma and Islam were the main religions.

Part 2

How did the Gurus teach about the Divine?

Theology · Equality in Sikhi

Key words

  • Sikhi (say: sick-ee): the name of the religion. It comes from Sikhna, meaning ‘to learn’.
  • Guru: an enlightener, who leads people from darkness into light.
  • Waheguru: the one divine being. It means ‘Wonderful Lord’ or ‘Wonderful Enlightener’.
  • Sikhs prefer the word divine rather than God.

The Mool Mantar

  • The Mool Mantar is the Sikh statement of belief.
  • Ik Onkar: there is one divine. The divine is in everything, and everything is the divine.
  • ‘His name is truth’: Sikhs meditate on the name Waheguru, repeating it, to reach the truth.
  • ‘No hate, no fear’: because the divine has no hate and no fear, Sikhs should treat all humans without hate or fear.
Remember: Ik Onkar: one divine, in everything. If the divine is in everyone, everyone is equal.

Part 3

Sewa: selfless service

Theology and Social Sciences · Equality in Sikhi

Key words

  • Sewa (say: say-va): selfless service, without expecting a reward.
  • Langar: a free kitchen in every gurdwara. Anyone can eat, whatever their religion.
  • Equality: treating people in a way that shows respect to them.
  • Guru Granth Sahib: the Sikh holy book, seen as a living Guru.
  • Gurdwara: ‘doorway to the Guru’, the building that houses the Guru Granth Sahib.

Equality in action

  • Guru Nanak said: “There is neither Hindu nor Muslim, and the path I follow is God’s path.”
  • In the langar, everyone sits together on the floor and eats the same food: rich and poor, any religion.
  • The Golden Temple (Harmandir Sahib) in Amritsar, India, has the largest free kitchen in the world.
Remember: Sewa = selfless service. Langar = free kitchen where everyone eats as equals.

Part 4

Gender equality in Sikhi

Theology and Social Sciences · Equality in Sikhi

Key ideas

  • Gender equality: women and men have the same rights and opportunities.
  • Sikhi teaches that the divine is in everyone, so women and men are equal.
  • Women can lead prayers, read from the Guru Granth Sahib and serve in the langar.

Using these examples

  • Each woman shows a Sikh value in action: sewa, courage, justice.
  • You may have met Princess Sophia in History, when you studied the suffragettes.
  • In an answer, give the example AND explain which Sikh teaching it shows.
Remember: Mata Khivi (langar), Mai Bhago (warrior), Princess Sophia Duleep Singh (suffragette).

Part 5

How was the Khalsa formed?

Theology and Social Sciences · Equality in Sikhi

Key words

  • Khalsa (say: kal-sa): initiated Sikhs. The word means ‘pure’.
  • Guru Gobind Singh: the tenth Guru, who introduced the Khalsa and the Five Ks in 1699, at the festival of Vaisakhi.
  • Amrit: the ceremony that initiates Sikhs into the Khalsa. Amrit is also the name of the sugar water used.

Why do the Five Ks matter?

  • They show a Sikh’s commitment to the Gurus’ teachings.
  • They make all Khalsa Sikhs look the same, which shows equality.
  • Each one has deeper symbolism, and some, like the kara and kirpan, were once used for protection.
Remember: Kesh, Kanga, Kara, Kachera, Kirpan: worn at all times by the Khalsa.

Part 6

What does it mean to be a Sikh in Britain?

Social Sciences · Equality in Sikhi

What the Census shows

  • The Census is a survey of everyone in the country, every 10 years.
  • In 2021, about 524,000 people in England and Wales said they were Sikh: 0.9% of the population.
  • This is up from about 423,000 (0.8%) in 2011, so the number of Sikhs is growing.
  • Sikhs have a younger age profile than the population as a whole, and the West Midlands has the biggest concentration.

Young Sikhs in the UK

  • Your lessons used the RE Today Sikh Youth Survey UK 2023: what young Sikhs think makes someone a Sikh, and which teachings matter most to them.
  • Being a Sikh can mean belief, practice, family, culture, or all of these.
  • Sikhs who wear a turban do not have to wear a motorcycle helmet by law, and are exempt from wearing a hard hat in most workplaces.
Remember: Census 2021: about 524,000 Sikhs in England and Wales (0.9%), up from 0.8% in 2011.

Part 7

Is there such a thing as right or wrong?

Philosophy · What is right and wrong?

Key words

  • Morality: a person’s principles of right behaviour, or the difference between right and wrong.
  • Moral absolutism: the belief that there is a universal set of moral rules for all situations.
  • Moral relativism: the idea that there is no universal or absolute set of moral rules.

What shapes our sense of right and wrong?

  • Culture
  • Religion
  • The law
  • An inner feeling, or conscience
  • Friends, teachers and parents
Remember: Absolutism = rules never change. Relativism = it depends on the situation or culture.

Part 8

Does morality come from God?

Philosophy · What is right and wrong?

Divine Command Theory

  • An action is morally good because God commands it.
  • An action is wrong because God forbids it.
  • It is an absolutist theory: God’s commands apply to everyone, always.
  • Obedience to God is the key moral principle.

Questions to think about

  • Does God command things because they are good, or are they good because God commands them?
  • What if two religions say God commands different things?
  • Can people without religion still know right from wrong?
Remember: Divine Command Theory: good = what God commands. It is absolutist.

Part 9

Where does the conscience come from?

Philosophy · What is right and wrong?

Key words

  • Conscience: an inner feeling or voice that guides us about whether our behaviour is right or wrong.
  • Superego: the part of the mind that holds our moral standards (Freud).
  • Grace: God’s unconditional love, given without needing to be earned (Augustine).
  • Non-reciprocal altruism: real kindness without expecting anything back (Dawkins).

Comparing the views

  • Augustine: inner conflict between our desires and our conscience comes from the struggle between good and evil within us.
  • Freud: conscience is learned, so different upbringings give different consciences.
  • Dawkins: humans are special because we can rise above our selfish instincts.
Remember: Augustine = voice of God. Freud = superego from upbringing. Dawkins = evolution.
